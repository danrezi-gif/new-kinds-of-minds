## 4. Full project description

Somewhere in Fortaleza there is an association of autistic adults who write their own manifesto. In the Ciudad Vieja of Montevideo, a radio station run by psychiatric patients and former patients has been broadcasting from the Hospital Vilardebó since 1997. In a university in Ribeirão Preto, people who hear voices meet weekly to compare notes. In Mexico City a collective of psychiatric survivors organizes under the name Chuhcan. In Santiago and Buenos Aires there are Mad Pride marches; in Lima, Bogotá, and Asunción, self-run associations of autistic people. Almost none of these places know about each other. None of them appear on the same list.

New Kinds of Minds is a map that puts them there. It is a participatory work of digital cartography that documents the organizations, collectives, artistic practices, research groups, schools, media, and peer communities through which societies are learning to treat cognitive difference as difference rather than defect. It is simultaneously an artwork, a public research instrument, and an open dataset, and it is built so that the people it depicts can write and correct their own entries.

**What exists now.** A first public version is live at new-kinds-of-minds-hub.vercel.app, with 102 verified initiatives in 47 countries, 33 of them in twelve Latin American countries. Every record carries the page it was checked against and the date, a plain-language description written in the atlas's own words, and three classifications: the initiative's *kind* (peer community, advocacy, arts and culture, research, education, work, care, media), its *focus* (autism, ADHD, dyslexia, Tourette, and the other familiar categories, and also the Mad Pride, psychiatric survivor, and Hearing Voices movements), and its *leadership*, recorded as neurodivergent-led only when the initiative says so itself. The map draws each kind with its own shape on paper-toned ground, so that a region reads as a pattern of forms. There is a full index for people who do not want a map, a permanent page for each entry, a public submission form with human review, and the whole dataset downloadable under CC BY 4.0 with its schema and taxonomy published and versioned. The code is open source.

**The artistic proposition.** A map is never only a record. It decides what is visible, at what scale, under which names, and in whose language, and those decisions make the object they claim to describe. Iconoclasistas in Buenos Aires turned this into a collective practice, the *mapeo colectivo*; Forensic Architecture turned it into evidence; Mark Lombardi drew networks of power as pencil constellations. New Kinds of Minds works in that tradition, with a subject that has never had a map: the emerging culture of neurodiversity, which exists as thousands of small institutions and vocabularies scattered across continents and mostly invisible to one another. The atlas gives that culture a face by drawing it, and the drawing is collaborative: the people mapped are invited to claim, correct, and expand their records in their own languages, and the taxonomy itself is revised in public with the communities it describes. The artwork is the accumulating portrait, and it is never finished.

**The research proposition.** Once enough verified initiatives are on the map, questions become askable that no single organization can answer from inside. Where is neurodiversity chiefly a matter for advocacy, and where for care? Which forms of neurodivergence have built institutions, and which have only a hashtag? Where are initiatives led by neurodivergent people themselves, and where on their behalf? Which vocabularies cross borders? Where is nothing recorded, and is that absence or invisibility? The philosopher Ian Hacking called it the looping effect: when a human kind is named, the people so named change, and the category changes with them. The atlas records those categories as they are used in 2026, admits that they are provisional, and keeps a history of their revision, because a record of how minds have been classified is part of what it is trying to show.

**Why Latin America first.** The word neurodiversity was coined in English in the late 1990s and travelled unevenly. In much of Latin America it met an older and larger tradition, the psychiatric reform and antimanicomial movements, and the two vocabularies still circle each other. Latin American neurodiversity work is abundant, mostly in Portuguese and Spanish, and largely absent from the directories and discourse that circulate in English. Global digital maps reproduce that asymmetry unless they are built against it. Phase I is built against it: a year of intensive mapping across Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Peru, Paraguay, Bolivia, Ecuador, and the Caribbean, documenting initiatives in their own languages and contexts, with regional contributors and an advisory circle drawn from the communities being mapped.

**Method.** The year has seven strands, described in the implementation plan: taxonomy refinement with the advisory circle; Latin American mapping by two paid regional contributors working from initiatives' own sources; platform and artistic development, including a Portuguese, Spanish, and English interface and two commissioned data-visual essays on the patterns that emerge; a participatory campaign in which initiatives claim and rewrite their records and contribute short descriptions in their own words; an accessibility review and implementation led by neurodivergent consultants; publication of the dataset as a citable open release with a read-only API; and a public program of three online gatherings among mapped initiatives, so that the connections the map draws become conversations.

**Social dimension.** The impact is visibility, recognition, and connection for initiatives that are usually isolated from one another: a self-run autistic association in Lima appearing beside its counterparts in Bogotá and Fortaleza; a hospital radio in Montevideo recorded as part of the same movement as a university research nucleus in Santiago; a family-led dyslexia foundation and an autistic-led collective in the same city seeing each other on the same map, with their differences named rather than erased. Honoraria go to neurodivergent collaborators. Testimonies and descriptions are written by participants. The atlas asks nothing of the initiatives it records except the chance to describe themselves, and it gives back an open dataset they can use for their own advocacy, research, and funding.

**Outputs.** At the end of the year: at least 300 verified Latin American initiatives within a global atlas of 500 or more; a trilingual, accessibility-reviewed interface; a participatory system with at least 40 community submissions and 20 records claimed by the initiatives themselves; two data-visual essays; three online gatherings; a citable open dataset release with API and documentation; and a documented account of the method, so that other regions can follow.

**Who.** I am a clinical psychologist and researcher, born in Buenos Aires, resident in Brazil, and a doctoral candidate in Religious Studies at Rice University. For twelve years I sat with clients whose minds did not fit the categories I had been trained to apply, and I stopped believing the categories were the interesting part. What people build around a difference is the interesting part, and it has addresses. I build interactive and immersive works about consciousness and unusual minds (Ontik, Quantum Sphere, Quantum I Ching), founded Entrementes, a bilingual project for neurodivergent people, artists, and therapists in Brazil, and have used the name New Kinds of Minds for this body of work since 2019. The atlas is its first public instrument.

## 5. Objectives

1. **Map Latin America.** Add at least 300 verified initiatives across at least twelve Latin American countries, each documented in Portuguese or Spanish with its source, bringing the global atlas past 500 entries.
2. **Make the atlas participatory.** Run a public contribution process with two paid regional contributors, an advisory circle of six neurodivergent collaborators, at least 40 community submissions, and at least 20 records claimed and rewritten by the initiatives themselves.
3. **Develop the work as art.** Release the atlas in Portuguese, Spanish, and English, extend the visual system, and commission two data-visual essays on the patterns the Latin American mapping reveals.
4. **Make it accessible.** Complete an accessibility review with neurodivergent consultants and implement its findings so that the core flows (map, index, entry, contribution) meet WCAG 2.2 AA.
5. **Open the knowledge.** Publish a citable dataset release (version 1.0) with a read-only API, documented schema and taxonomy, and a record of taxonomy revisions.
6. **Connect the mapped.** Convene three online gatherings among mapped initiatives, one in Portuguese, one in Spanish, one bilingual, and publish what was said.

## 6. Implementation plan and timeline (January to December 2027)

The project runs twelve months from the award, in seven strands that overlap.

**Strand 1. Taxonomy and advisory circle (January to February).** Recruit six advisors from mapped Latin American initiatives (autistic-led, Mad Pride and antimanicomial, dyslexia and ADHD, research, arts), paid honoraria. Two working sessions to revise the taxonomy, the leadership field, and the inclusion criteria. Publish taxonomy version 0.2 with a changelog.

**Strand 2. Latin American mapping (February to July).** Two regional contributors, one for Brazil and one for Spanish-speaking Latin America, paid for roughly six months of part-time work, research and verify initiatives country by country from their own websites and the press and academic literature around them. Target: 300 verified entries, at least twelve countries, with attention to initiatives outside capital cities, to Indigenous and Afro-descendant contexts, and to initiatives that do not use the word neurodiversity at all. Every entry checked by the project lead before publication.

**Strand 3. Platform and artistic development (March to September).** Interface in Portuguese, Spanish, and English; summaries in the initiative's own language; a network field so that federations and their members can be drawn as links; visual essays. Two data-visual essays commissioned from the emerging patterns (for example, the geography of neurodivergent-led versus family-led initiatives, or the relation between antimanicomial and neurodiversity vocabularies across the continent), published on the site and shared with the initiatives depicted.

**Strand 4. Participation (April to October).** Invitation campaign to every mapped Latin American initiative to claim its record, correct it, and write its own description; public submission form promoted through the advisory circle's networks; short testimonies from participants published with consent. Targets: 40 community submissions, 20 claimed records.

**Strand 5. Accessibility and localization (June to September).** Review by a neurodivergent-led accessibility consultancy covering keyboard navigation, screen readers, cognitive load, motion, and plain language in all three languages; implementation of findings; public accessibility statement that says what was done and what remains.

**Strand 6. Publication (September to November).** Dataset release version 1.0 with a DOI, a read-only API, documentation for researchers, and per-record history. Announcement to research groups on disability studies and mad studies in the region.

**Strand 7. Public program, documentation, evaluation (October to December).** Three online gatherings among mapped initiatives (Portuguese, Spanish, bilingual), with captioning and interpretation, recorded and summarized. Documentation of the method as a short video and a written account. Evaluation against the targets above, and a final report to the Foundation.

## 7. Justification of interest (why this grant, why this Foundation)

Fundación Ama Amoedo's grants exist to strengthen the Latin American art ecosystem, and the Art and Social Engagement category supports initiatives that use artistic practice for education, inclusion, and community. New Kinds of Minds fits that description without being bent to it. Its medium is participatory cartography, a practice with a specifically Latin American lineage in the collective mapping of Iconoclasistas and the counter-cartographies of the region's social movements. Its subject is a community that the art world rarely sees as a culture: neurodivergent people, and the institutions they and their allies have built. And its method is collaborative by construction: the map is drawn by the people on it.

The Foundation's 2025 grantees in this category, Alianza Wichí in Argentina and Nube Lab in Chile, both work at the meeting point of artistic practice and communities that are usually spoken about rather than listened to. This project shares that commitment. What it adds is scale and durability: an open, citable dataset that keeps growing after the grant ends, and a public artwork that makes an entire emerging movement perceptible for the first time.

The grant would fund the part of the project that cannot be done alone and should not be done unpaid: the regional contributors, the neurodivergent advisors and collaborators, the accessibility review, the translations, and the gatherings. The platform already exists. What is missing is the year of collective work that turns a prototype with 33 Latin American entries into the continent's atlas of itself.

## 8. Artistic relevance

Cartography is an art of decisions. A map chooses a projection, a scale, a set of names, a legend, and each choice makes the territory it claims only to describe. Brian Harley made that argument about state maps in 1989; Iconoclasistas in Buenos Aires turned it into a collective practice, handing the pens to neighborhoods and social movements; Forensic Architecture turned it into evidence; Mark Lombardi drew the networks of finance and power as pencil constellations that could be exhibited and read. New Kinds of Minds belongs to this line of work. Its subject has never had a map.

The atlas is an artwork in three ways. It is a **drawing**: each kind of initiative has its own mark, eight shapes on paper-toned ground, so that a city or a continent reads as a pattern of forms and not as a rainbow of pins. It is a **collective authorship**: the people mapped write their own entries, in their own languages, and argue about the categories, and the taxonomy is revised in public with a visible history. And it is a **portrait**: not of any one person or organization, but of a species in the middle of changing its mind about what a mind is allowed to be. The image accumulates over years; it is never finished, and its incompleteness is part of what it shows.

The work belongs in an exhibition space, a research lab, and a public-interest technology conference at once, and it has been designed to hold in all three. What it borrows from data art is rigor about sources; what it borrows from socially engaged practice is that the participants are co-authors, not subjects; what it borrows from cartography is the knowledge that visibility is a decision, and the decision is the work.

## 9. Social relevance

The neurodiversity movement is dispersed across countries, diagnoses, disciplines, and languages. An autistic-led association in Lima, a psychiatric-patient radio in Montevideo, a dyslexia foundation in São Paulo, a Hearing Voices group in Ribeirão Preto, and a Mad Pride collective in Santiago are all working on the same question, and none of them appear on the same list. Most are invisible beyond their own city, their own diagnosis, or their own language. That invisibility has consequences: isolation, duplicated effort, funders and researchers who cannot find them, and a public conversation about neurodiversity that is conducted in English about English-speaking countries.

The atlas makes the ecosystem perceptible, and it does so with a discipline the subject requires. It records who leads each initiative, because the difference between an autistic-led collective and a parent-led foundation is the argument the movement itself is having, and the map should show it rather than settle it. It includes the Mad Pride and antimanicomial traditions, which in Latin America are the older and larger movement, and which share the neurodiversity argument even where they refuse the word. It writes plain descriptions rather than promotional ones. And it is built to be used by the people on it: for finding each other, for advocacy, for research, and for funding applications of their own.

Because it is a neurodiversity project, accessibility is not decoration. The map is one view; everything is also an index, a table, and a plain page. The interface respects reduced-motion preferences, keyboard navigation, and screen readers, and the funded year includes a review by neurodivergent consultants and a public statement of what was and was not achieved.

## 10. Latin American relevance

Global digital maps and neurodiversity discourse reproduce the visibility of the English-speaking Global North. The largest directories are national and Anglophone; the academic literature is overwhelmingly in English; the word itself is an English coinage. Latin America has an abundance of the work the atlas is about, and most of it is invisible from outside the region.

It also has something the Anglophone movement lacks: a longer history. Brazil's Luta Antimanicomial has been closing asylums since the 1980s under the influence of Franco Basaglia; Argentina's 2010 mental health law came out of the same tradition; Uruguay's Radio Vilardevoz has been run by psychiatric patients since 1997; Mad Pride marches in Buenos Aires and Santiago predate much of the region's autism self-advocacy. When neurodiversity arrived, it met this tradition, and the encounter between the two vocabularies is one of the most interesting things happening anywhere in the movement. No map shows it.

Phase I documents Latin American initiatives in Portuguese and Spanish, in their own terms, with regional contributors from the communities mapped and an advisory circle drawn from them. The first 33 Latin American entries already show the range: ABRAÇA in Fortaleza and the autistic collectives of USP and UFRGS; the Movimento Nacional da Luta Antimanicomial in Salvador; Mad in Brasil; Orgullo Loco in Buenos Aires and Santiago; Colectivo Chuhcan and Autistas de México in Mexico City; AUTISCOL in Bogotá; APEAU in Lima; ASO TEA in Asunción; Aletea, RUATDAH, and Radio Vilardevoz in Montevideo; the Millennium Nucleus on Disability and Citizenship in Santiago. The year would take that to 300, reaching beyond capital cities and into contexts, Indigenous and Afro-descendant among them, where cognitive difference is described without diagnostic language at all.

The result is a Latin American atlas of itself, inside a global one, published in the region's languages, and owned by nobody.

## 12. Expected impact

Qualitative: initiatives that did not know of each other find each other; initiatives see their own leadership and vocabulary named accurately rather than absorbed into a generic category; researchers and funders in the region gain a verified, citable source; the public conversation about neurodiversity gains a Latin American picture drawn in the region's languages.

Quantitative targets (targets, not promises): 300 Latin American entries; twelve countries; six paid advisors; two paid contributors; 40 submissions; 20 claimed records; three gatherings with at least 60 participants in total; 5,000 unique visitors in the year; at least five citations or reuses of the dataset by researchers, journalists, or organizations.

## 13. Sustainability

The infrastructure is deliberately small. The site is a static export served for free; the dataset is a file in a public repository; the submissions service runs on a server that costs twelve dollars a month and is shared with other projects. After the grant, the atlas continues to accept submissions, the dataset keeps its value and grows with every correction, and the code and data are open under MIT and CC BY 4.0 so that they survive the project's author. The taxonomy and method are documented so that other regions can follow the Latin American phase. Research collaborations and further grants (disability studies, digital humanities, open data) are the natural next funders, and a citable dataset release makes those conversations easier. The project's founder has maintained comparable works (Ontik, Quantum I Ching, Entrementes) for years without external funding; this grant buys the collective year, not the survival of the platform.
